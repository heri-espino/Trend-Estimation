\section{Related work and positioning}

\subsection{Finite-difference smoothing and the meaning of smoothness}

Whittaker's graduation method \cite{whittaker1923} and subsequent
finite-difference penalized least-squares (PLS) formulations establish
the estimator underlying this paper. Guerrero \cite{guerrero2007penalized}
derives a statistical interpretation of PLS and a trace-based index of
trend smoothness; Guerrero \cite{guerrero2008smoothness} uses that index
to select a penalty corresponding to a smoothness percentage specified
by the analyst. The basic smoother and the idea of measuring its
effective smoothness are therefore established methods, not proposed
here as new estimators.

Of particular relevance to the intended journal, Cort\'es-Toto et
al.\ \cite{cortes2017smoothness} compare the smoothness attained by
CV, GCV, AICc, and BIC in finite-difference PLS. They show that
attained smoothness varies with sample length and trend specification.
The present normalized index is a rescaling of the established
trace-based quantity: it removes the unpenalized null-space degrees
of freedom so that the maximum is one for a fixed $(d,L)$. Neither
this monotone rescaling nor optimizing over it creates a different
class of trend estimates.

Serial dependence matters as well. Guerrero et
al.\ \cite{guerrero2018autocorr} explicitly study AR(1) noise and
its effect on controlled-smoothness trend estimation. Our baseline
retains an identity-weight PLS estimator and treats dependent noise
as a simulation mechanism, not as an explicit covariance model.
The distinction limits any claim of robustness to serial dependence.

The classical controlled-smoothness direction is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:related_controlled_smoothness}
\text{user chooses }S\longrightarrow\lambda(S).
\end{equation}
A forecast-oriented procedure instead uses
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:related_forecast_smoothness}
\text{chronological forecast loss}\longrightarrow S^\star.
\end{equation}
Forecast-based selection is not new in general. The principal proposal here
is a fully specified chronological $h$-step forecast-MSE criterion
for normalized PLS smoothness, with a final refit on the most recent
window. Temporal tracking of local minima is an optional adaptation
mechanism, not a prerequisite of forecast-CV.

\subsection{Controlled trends used for forecasting}

Forecasting with a controlled-smoothness trend has clear prior
applications. Guerrero and Galicia-V\'azquez
\cite{guerrero2010financial} study trend estimation for financial
series through a smoothing filter with an associated PLS
interpretation. Islas-Camargo, Guerrero, and Silva
\cite{islas2019remittances} forecast Mexican remittances with a
Markov-switching model applied to a controlled-smoothness trend.
Islas-Camargo and Zumaya-Galv\'an \cite{islas2025exchange}
similarly study exchange-rate forecasts using a trend with
controlled smoothness. These articles establish the substantive
precedent for using this class of filters in forecasting, although
their forecasting specifications differ from direct, pooled
$h$-step MSE selection over normalized PLS smoothness.

Taylor \cite{taylor2004stes} provides a related but distinct example
of forecast-error-based tuning in exponential smoothing, including
time-varying smoothing weights. Zafar et al.\
\cite{zafar2022filter} compare filtering methods by the subsequent
short-horizon forecasts. In contrast, we keep the finite-difference
PLS estimator fixed and study variation in the selection of its
smoothness parameter.

\subsection{Predictive validation and numerical smoothing selection}

The distinction between fitting a historical signal and selecting
a tuning parameter for prediction is well established. Hart
\cite{hart1994tscv} studies time-series cross-validation for kernel
smoothing under dependent errors. In this journal, Vilar-Fern\'andez
and Cao \cite{vilar2007nonparametric} compare nonparametric
time-series forecasting methods and address smoothing-parameter
selection for dependent observations. They select tuning
parameters for nonparametric autoregressions rather than for
finite-difference trend continuation, but share the predictive
motivation.

GCV is another important benchmark: the classical formulation
of Craven and Wahba \cite{craven1979gcv} and the GCVPACK
computational framework of Bates et al.\
\cite{bates1987gcvpack}, also published in this journal, concern
selection of regularization or smoothing parameters across
linear smoothing problems. Their criteria are not equivalent
to our chronologically refitted $h$-step forecast loss.

Tashman \cite{tashman2000oos} surveys forecast evaluation using
held-out time blocks; Bergmeir and Ben\'itez \cite{bergmeir2012cv}
examine validation choices for time-series predictors; Bergmeir et al.\
\cite{bergmeir2018validity} identify conditions under which standard
cross-validation can be valid for autoregressive prediction; and
Stan\v{e}k \cite{stanek2023oos} studies estimation of out-of-sample
loss. In the present setting, all parameter-selection information
must precede an untouched test block. Its forecast horizon,
estimation window, continuation rule, and update schedule are
part of the statistical procedure.

\subsection{Recent PLS developments and endpoint limitations}

Recent work reinforces the need to specify what the smoothing
parameter is optimized \emph{for}. Franke, Kukacka, and Sacht
\cite{franke2026hp} select Hodrick--Prescott penalties in
simulations designed to approximate latent growth trends;
that is a different loss target from future prediction.
Biessy \cite{biessy2026whittaker} revisits Whittaker--Henderson
smoothing with marginal-likelihood parameter selection and
constrained extrapolation. Kim et al.\ \cite{kim2009l1}
replace the quadratic trend penalty with an $\ell_1$ penalty
that encourages piecewise-linear fits, a different estimator
from the quadratic PLS considered here.

Hamilton \cite{hamilton2018hp} highlights endpoint and spurious
dynamics problems of the Hodrick--Prescott filter. These concerns
are germane because our forecasts extrapolate a trend fitted at
the sample boundary. Optimizing the penalty for a future loss
does not by itself remove extrapolation instability; the
high-difference-order findings reported later in this paper
make this limitation explicit.

\subsection{Specific contribution and boundary}

The PLS smoother, controlled-smoothness index, and predictive
cross-validation all have clear antecedents. We therefore do not
claim any of them as an independent invention. Our central
methodological construction applies chronological forecast MSE
at the \emph{intended horizon} directly to the smoothness
parameter of a finite-difference PLS trend, with an explicit
continuation operator and an operational final refit. Its
performance is compared with one-step forecast tuning and
classical criteria for the smoothing penalty.

The objective can be multimodal. The companion numerical-methods
paper addresses stationary-point discovery, Brent refinement,
and endpoint comparison; Sturm-based certification remains
exploratory. The forecasting paper additionally illustrates
time-adaptive selection from tracked histories of local minima.
This optional extension requires assumptions about how
past preferred smoothing regimes inform future choices;
it is not the defining contribution of the pooled criterion.
