\section{Conclusion}

In penalized trend estimation, smoothness is usually represented by a
single regularization parameter. The normalized smoothness coordinate
offers an interpretable alternative, but chronological forecast-loss
surfaces may admit several local minima whose locations vary across
forecast origins. Treating these minima as unrelated tuning outcomes
discards their temporal structure.

This paper formulates that structure through tracked branches. For each
branch, a history of smoothness values and successive Validation-1 and
Validation-2 forecast losses defines a state $V_j$. The decomposition
\[
\widehat j_T=\psi(V_1,\ldots,V_J),
\qquad
\widehat S_T=\phi(V_{\widehat j_T})
\]
separates selecting a historically credible branch from deciding how its
information should produce today's smoothness. The map $\phi$ need not
be unique: the newest minimum, convex recency averages, predictive-loss
weights, and extrapolations of the smoothness trajectory are all
well-defined examples. This family of decisions is the central
methodological contribution, not the identification of a universally
optimal smoothing rule.

The empirical record sets clear limits on forecasting claims.
Horizon-matched pooled forecast-CV is useful in controlled simulation.
The frozen recency-based branch rule improves on pooled CV in one small
confirmation panel, but does not retain that advantage on a broader
financial panel or in the simulation with changing latent roughness.
High-order polynomial continuation also introduces a stability
problem that is separate from tracking itself. Finally, the
trajectory-rule demonstrations reveal meaningful differences in
boundary behavior and forecast error, without establishing superiority
for the extrapolative rules.

After every smoothness decision, the operational trend is estimated
afresh on the latest available data; historical validation fits are
never averaged into the forecast. This chronology makes the proposed
branch representation usable for future decision rules and empirical
settings while preserving the distinction between a flexible
forecasting framework and an empirically validated predictive gain.
The companion numerical study addresses the separate task of
recovering local minima and maintaining their correspondence through
time.
