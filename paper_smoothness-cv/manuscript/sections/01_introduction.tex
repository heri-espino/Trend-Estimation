\section{Introduction}

Smoothing parameters are often treated as technical constants, although in forecasting they determine a substantive choice: how much recent movement is treated as signal and how much is suppressed before extrapolation. Finite-difference penalized least squares (PLS) makes this trade-off especially transparent because the penalty can be translated into an interpretable normalized smoothness scale.

Guerrero \cite{guerrero2007penalized,guerrero2008smoothness} develops this controlled-smoothness view. The forecasting problem begins once smoothness is no longer specified ex ante. A natural first approach is to choose the value that minimizes historical chronological forecast loss. That pooled selector remains an important baseline in this paper, but it ignores a feature that becomes visible when the loss surface is inspected repeatedly through time: the same surface can contain several local minima, and those minima can persist, drift, appear, or disappear across neighboring forecast origins.

The paper therefore studies forecast-optimal smoothness dynamically. At each chronological origin $t$, let
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:intro_local_minimum_set}
\mathcal M_t=\{S_{1,t},\ldots,S_{K_t,t}\}
\end{equation}
be the local minima of the origin-specific forecast-loss surface. Nearby minima on adjacent surfaces are connected into data-driven branches. A branch is represented by its smoothness trajectory together with the Validation-1 loss at which the local minimum is identified and the subsequent Validation-2 forecast loss obtained after refitting through Validation 1.

This produces a branch state
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:intro_branch_state}
V_j=[S_{j,t},\ell^{(1)}_{j,t},\ell^{(2)}_{j,t}]_t.
\end{equation}
The forecasting decision then has two stages. First, a historical rule $\psi$ selects a persistent branch. Second, a rule $\phi$ maps the selected branch history into the current smoothness:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:intro_branch_decision}
\widehat j_T=\psi(V_1,\ldots,V_J),
\qquad
\widehat S_T=\phi(V_{\widehat j_T}).
\end{equation}

The simplest dynamic rule uses the newest tracked minimum. Other admissible maps can use a recent mean or median, Validation-2-weighted smoothness, recency weights, or forecasts of the smoothness trajectory itself. They operate on the same branch history; the purpose of presenting several examples is to make the decision space explicit, not to define a universal best rule.

The older pooled criterion remains useful as a benchmark. For historical origins $T_1,\ldots,T_M$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:intro_pooled_loss}
F^{\mathrm{pool}}_{d,L,h}(S)
=
\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}F_{T_m}(S),
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:intro_pooled_choice}
\widehat S^{\mathrm{pool}}_{d,L,h}
\in\arg\min_S F^{\mathrm{pool}}_{d,L,h}(S).
\end{equation}
It answers which single smoothness value has the lowest average historical forecast loss. The dynamic framework retains the temporal organization of multiple local optima and makes it available to a family of smoothness decisions. Whether any particular decision improves future forecasts remains an empirical question.

The forecasting literature provides strong precedents for choosing tuning parameters by predictive performance. Forecast-error minimization is standard in exponential smoothing \cite{taylor2004stes}; Hart \cite{hart1994tscv} studies predictive smoothing selection in dependent data; and more recent forecasting work continues to connect tuning and out-of-sample loss \cite{zafar2022filter,stanek2023oos,wolff2024stock,franjic2025predictor,xu2025vix}. The present contribution is narrower: it focuses on the normalized smoothness of finite-difference penalized trends and the temporal evolution of multiple minima of their forecast-loss surfaces.

The distinction from numerical optimization is also explicit. The companion numerical paper studies how to recover all relevant local minima of each surface and how to correspond them across time. This paper studies what those tracked branches mean for forecasting and how they should be converted into the current smoothness.

Finally, validation never supplies the trend fit used in the final forecast. Once $\widehat S_T$ is chosen, all temporary validation fits are discarded, the trend is refit on the newest $L$ observations available at $T$, and only then is the untouched future block forecast.

The empirical record is informative but mixed. Horizon-matched pooled selection improves forecast accuracy in the controlled baseline simulation. A frozen branch-based recency rule performs favorably in a small confirmation panel but not in a broader financial panel; simulations under changing latent roughness also fail to establish a general improvement over pooled forecast-CV. The principal contribution is thus the explicit separation between recovering and tracking multiple forecast-loss minima, selecting a branch, and defining a current smoothness decision from that branch. The remainder of the paper develops these objects, reports the frozen empirical results, and illustrates several distinct decision maps under common information restrictions.
