\section{Introduction}

The smoothness of an estimated trend is not automatically the
smoothness that yields the best forecast. In finite-difference
penalized least squares (PLS), the penalty $\lambda$ controls the
trade-off between fidelity to observed data and regularity of a
fitted historical path. If the purpose is extrapolation, its
smoothness should instead be evaluated by predictive accuracy at
the intended horizon $h$. A trend that recovers the latent signal
well need not have a stable terminal extrapolation.

The PLS smoother and the smoothness index are established by the
work of Guerrero \cite{guerrero2007penalized,guerrero2008smoothness},
while Cort\'es-Toto et al.\ \cite{cortes2017smoothness} analyze the
smoothness induced by conventional model-selection criteria.
For a length-$L$ training window $x_t$, let
$H_\lambda=(I+\lambda D_d^\top D_d)^{-1}$ be its smoothing matrix
and let $S(\lambda)\in[0,1]$ denote a normalized trace-based
smoothness index. Let $G_{d,h}$ extrapolate the fitted trend over
$h$ future observations using its declared finite-difference
continuation rule.

The paper's principal proposal is to select smoothness by
chronological, horizon-specific forecast cross-validation. For
historical forecast origins $t_1,\ldots,t_M$, define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:intro_pooled_loss}
F^{\mathrm{pool}}_{d,L,h}(S)
=
\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^M\frac1h
\left\|y_{t_m+1:t_m+h}
-G_{d,h}H_{\lambda(S)}x_{t_m}\right\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
Then select
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:intro_pooled_choice}
\widehat S^{\mathrm{FCV}}_{T,d,L,h}
\in\arg\min_{S\in[0,1]}
F^{\mathrm{pool}}_{d,L,h}(S).
\end{equation}
Every validation block used in \cref{eq:intro_pooled_loss}
has been observed by the outer origin $T$. The future test
observations after $T$ never enter smoothness selection.

This is a complete procedure without a time-varying branch state.
Its defining choices are the smoothing matrix $H_\lambda$, the
normalized smoothness parameter, a fixed extrapolation rule, and
historical mean squared \emph{forecast} error computed at the
intended horizon. Once smoothness has been selected, all validation
fits are discarded; the trend is refitted using the latest
$L$ observations and extrapolated into the untouched test block.
The paper does not introduce PLS, normalized smoothness, or
predictive cross-validation in isolation. Forecast-based smoothing
selection has precedents in Hart \cite{hart1994tscv} and
Vilar-Fern\'andez and Cao \cite{vilar2007nonparametric};
controlled-smoothness trends have been used in prediction by
Islas-Camargo et al.\ \cite{islas2019remittances} and
Islas-Camargo and Zumaya-Galv\'an \cite{islas2025exchange}.
Our focus is their particular combination into a directly
horizon-matched PLS trend-extrapolation objective and
its comparison with established alternatives.

A numerical complication is that the forecast-MSE surface may
have multiple local minima. The companion numerical-methods
paper investigates adaptive stationary-point discovery, Brent
root refinement, and explicit endpoint comparisons. Potential
certification through rational structure and Sturm sequences
is exploratory; it is not a completed feature of the solver.

An optional extension treats the desirable smoothness as a
time-varying hyperparameter. If the analyst assumes that
recent smoothing regimes carry information about the next
forecast, historical local minima can be tracked into
branch states $V_j$, with rules for choosing a branch and
converting its history into the current smoothness.
The resulting maps embody different assumptions about
recency, persistence, and predictive relevance. They are
not necessary for \cref{eq:intro_pooled_choice} and are
not guaranteed to outperform it.

The empirical analysis therefore distinguishes the main
method from its extension. A frozen 3,000-scenario simulation
evaluates horizon-matched pooled forecast-CV against one-step
forecast tuning and classical criteria over 72,000
outer-origin--horizon decisions. Further financial and
changing-roughness experiments evaluate branch-based rules,
whose predictive advantages are not uniform. The primary
contribution is a forecast-targeted methodology for choosing
PLS trend smoothness; temporal branch adaptation is a
secondary, assumption-dependent use of the same objective.
